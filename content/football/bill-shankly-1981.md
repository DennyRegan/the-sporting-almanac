When Bill Shankly died on 29 September 1981, Liverpool were the reigning European champions. Bob Paisley had won the club’s third European Cup in May. Shankly, who had retired seven years earlier, had never lifted that trophy himself. The distance between the Liverpool he found and the Liverpool that now held it was the measure of his work.

He arrived in December 1959 to manage a club in the Second Division. His first match ended in a 4–0 home defeat by Cardiff City. Anfield needed repair; so did the playing staff. Shankly changed personnel and methods, building around the coaching group that included Paisley and Joe Fagan and recruiting players such as Ron Yeats and Ian St John. Liverpool won promotion in 1962 and the league championship two seasons later.

That rise did not happen in a single burst. Shankly’s first team won the FA Cup for Liverpool in 1965, then another league title in 1966. As those players aged, he built again. The next side, with Kevin Keegan and Ray Clemence among its leading figures, won the league and UEFA Cup in 1973. A 3–0 FA Cup final win over Newcastle followed in 1974. Shankly retired that summer after 14 and a half years in charge.

By then he had brought Liverpool three top-flight titles, two FA Cups and their first European trophy. Just as consequential was the continuity he left. Paisley had worked alongside him for years before taking the manager’s job. The routines at Melwood, the close coaching staff and the expectation that Liverpool should compete for major honours survived a change that might have broken a less settled club.

The news of Shankly’s death, after a heart attack, travelled far beyond Anfield. He was 68. A contemporary report recorded his wife Nessie at his bedside at Liverpool’s Broadgreen Hospital; Paisley spoke of his shock. Supporters lined the streets for the funeral. At Anfield, a banner later declared that Shankly would live forever. The phrase could sound too large anywhere else. It made sense to people who had watched a club change around them.

The familiar anecdotes and quotations can sometimes hide the practical manager. He signed players, changed training, built two successful sides and chose staff capable of carrying the work on. The European Cup belonged to Paisley and his players, and they earned it. Shankly’s part was to have made a Liverpool team capable of pursuing it. On the day he died, that ambition was no longer a promise. It was the club’s present.
