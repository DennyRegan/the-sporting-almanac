The Chile–Soviet Union play-off began with a match and ended without a second one. On 26 September 1973, Chile drew 0–0 at Moscow’s Lenin Stadium in the first leg of a tie for a place at the following year’s World Cup. Fifteen days earlier, a military coup had overthrown Chilean president Salvador Allende. The proposed venue for the return match had already become a matter of international dispute.

Chile’s squad had travelled through Mexico after the coup, and defender Elías Figueroa joined them in Europe. At the Mexico stop, Carlos Caszely and Francisco Valdés faced questions about events at home. Chile had eliminated Peru to reach this play-off; the Soviet side had eliminated France and were European Championship runners-up. They had Oleg Blokhin, who would win the Ballon d’Or in 1975. Chile’s task was to take a result back to Santiago against one of the strongest national sides outside the World Cup field.

The scoreless draw was hard earned. Soviet players later described a Chilean team defending with determination and a fine performance from goalkeeper Juan Olivares. Their side had been left without a lead before a return whose location was already in dispute. No known moving-image record exists by which to check their memories. A Russian television retrospective, reported by the Chilean journalist Aldo Schiappacasse, said the Kremlin had ordered the game neither broadcast nor recorded. A few photographs and the testimony of participants have had to carry the account of the 90 minutes.

The return was due at Santiago’s Estadio Nacional on 21 November. In September the stadium had been turned into a detention and torture centre by the new regime. Chile’s National Library records the confinement of thousands of people there after the coup. The Soviet authorities refused to send their team to play in that ground and sought a neutral venue. FIFA kept the fixture in Santiago.

Prisoners had been removed from the stadium before the appointed date. The Soviet players did not appear. Chile’s team walked onto the field without opponents, and Francisco Valdés scored into an empty goal in a symbolic sequence. Chile took the World Cup place, but that staged moment was not the return match the two sets of footballers had qualified to play.

The Moscow draw has sometimes been called the “ghost match” because no broadcast survives. The name can obscure the fact that a real match took place, under pressure the scoreline cannot express. Chile’s players earned a draw against formidable opposition. The tie that followed was decided by a refusal to use a stadium whose terraces and rooms had held prisoners. On 26 September, football and the coup were already part of the same story.
