Portsmouth and Reading had each scored twice by the 49th minute at Fratton Park. The match had already been lively; it was about to become a Premier League record. By the final whistle on 29 September 2007, Portsmouth had won 7–4. Eleven goals had been scored, more than in any other match in the competition’s history.

Benjani had put Harry Redknapp’s Portsmouth ahead early and scored again before half-time. Reading were not yet out of it. Stephen Hunt reduced the deficit just before the interval and Dave Kitson made it 2–2 soon after the restart. Those two goals mattered to the shape of the afternoon: this was not a procession in which one side kept adding to a helpless opponent’s score. Reading repeatedly found a way back, even as their defence kept giving Portsmouth another opening.

Hermann Hreiðarsson restored the home lead. At 3–2, Reading had a chance to draw level once more from the penalty spot, but David James saved Nicky Shorey’s kick. The save divided the match. Benjani completed his hat-trick, Niko Kranjčar made it 5–2, and the possibility of another Reading recovery began to look remote.

Still the scoring did not stop. Shane Long pulled one back. An own goal by Reading’s Ivar Ingimarsson took Portsmouth to six; Sulley Muntari scored a penalty in stoppage time for seven. Then Sol Campbell turned Shorey’s shot into his own net. Even at 7–4 the game was still producing a final twist, though by then the points were settled.

The record is easy to repeat and harder to picture. Benjani’s hat-trick, a goalkeeper’s penalty save, two own goals and eight second-half goals all had to fit into one league afternoon. Reading scored four away from home and lost by three. Portsmouth moved up the table and went on to win the FA Cup later that season, but this was no preview of a tidy cup run. It was an unusually unruly day in a season that would bring both clubs very different endings.

Reading finished the campaign relegated on goal difference; Portsmouth lifted the cup at Wembley. Neither outcome was clear on this September afternoon. What the crowd could know was that each apparent stopping point kept giving way to another goal. The final score remains singular in the Premier League: not because every goal was beautiful, but because a match that ought to have settled several times kept refusing to do so.
